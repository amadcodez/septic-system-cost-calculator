Arkansas unique system

The Designated Representative — Arkansas's key professional

Like Maine's Licensed Site Evaluator, Arkansas uses a single licensed professional who handles both soil evaluation and system design. No DR means no permit.

What a DR does

Soil evaluation + system design

The Designated Representative conducts a soil morphology evaluation on your site, determines what system type is feasible, sizes the components, and submits the design to the local county health unit for permitting. This is the first professional you hire — before an installer, before ordering any materials.

Who qualifies as a DR

Act 402 licensed professionals

Qualified DRs under Act 402 include: Registered Professional Engineers, Registered Professional Land Surveyors, Registered Professional Sanitarians, and Licensed Master Plumbers. Individuals with soil science education and equivalent experience may be approved on a case-by-case basis by ADH.

Finding a DR

ADH licensee database

Arkansas Department of Health maintains a searchable database of licensed Designated Representatives. Search by "Designated Representative" license type to find a DR in your county. Local DRs familiar with your county sanitarian and regional soil conditions are worth seeking out — they know what the local office expects in a permit application.

DR exam

Nine exam dates per year

The ADH administers the DR licensing examination nine times per year, January through September. Exam dates are posted on the ADH Training and Examination page. The same examination applies to anyone seeking DR status — including engineers and sanitarians who must pass it to practice in Arkansas. This is the same exam pathway as the installer license, administered separately.

Arkansas geology

Karst limestone, Ozark bedrock, and Delta clay — three different design problems

Arkansas's geology splits into three distinct zones. Each one creates a fundamentally different septic challenge, and the system type that works in one zone typically cannot be used in another.

Ozark Plateau

Fractured limestone — karst terrain

Northern and western Arkansas — Benton, Carroll, Boone, Marion, Stone, and Searcy counties — sit on the Ozark Plateau, where limestone and dolomite bedrock has been dissolved and fractured over millennia into karst terrain. Sinkholes, caves, and springs characterize this landscape. Conventional drainfield trenches cannot be used because effluent reaches fractured bedrock without adequate soil treatment and flows directly into groundwater. The ADH requires secondary treatment systems here: aerobic treatment units, sand filters, or mound systems that treat effluent thoroughly before it contacts the ground.

Ouachita Mountains

Thin rocky soil over sandstone and shale

The Ouachita range in west-central Arkansas — Polk, Montgomery, Garland, and surrounding counties — has thin soil over sandstone and shale bedrock. Unlike karst limestone, the Ouachita bedrock is not soluble and does not form caves, but shallow depth to rock still limits trench depth. Mound systems and shallow-trench designs with secondary treatment are common answers in this terrain. Depth to bedrock varies significantly within short distances, making site-by-site evaluation essential.

Mississippi Delta

Heavy clay — large fields required

Eastern Arkansas — the Delta counties along the Mississippi River — has deep alluvial clay soils with very slow percolation rates. Bedrock is not the problem here; absorption is. Heavy clay requires large drainfield areas to handle the same daily flow that sandy soil absorbs in a fraction of the space. Systems in Delta counties tend to be larger and more expensive than the same bedroom count in central Arkansas, due to the required field size.

Getting an Arkansas septic system permitted

Arkansas requires two permits — construction and operation — both from the local county health unit. The Designated Representative handles the design submission; the county sanitarian reviews and issues permits.

Step 1 — Hire a Designated Representative

Your first call. The DR conducts the soil morphology evaluation on your specific site, determines what system type ADH will approve, sizes the tank and drainfield, and prepares the permit application. Find a licensed DR through the ADH licensee database. Cost: $500 to $1,500 depending on site complexity and location.

Step 2 — Soil evaluation

The DR digs test pits and reads the soil profile: texture, structure, color, depth to restrictive layers (rock or water table), and percolation characteristics. In Ozark karst areas, this step determines whether any soil-absorption system is feasible at all. If the site is in a karst zone, the DR must document why a conventional system is not feasible and propose an alternative — this statement is required by ADH for alternative system applications.

Step 3 — Construction permit

The DR submits the design to the local county health unit. The county sanitarian reviews for compliance with ADH rules and issues the construction permit. No excavation or installation may begin before this permit is in hand. Review times vary by county — rural counties may take longer during spring and summer peak season.

Step 4 — Installation by a licensed installer

Arkansas requires a licensed Advanced Septic Installer for all installations. Homeowners who want to install their own system must pass the same ADH licensing examination — administered nine times per year — and register with the state before beginning work. For alternative systems in karst areas, homeowner installation is not permitted under any circumstances; a licensed installer is required.

Step 5 — Inspection and operation permit

After installation, the county sanitarian inspects before backfill. Once the system passes inspection, the operation permit is issued. Both permits must be in hand before the system is put into use. Inspection before backfill is non-negotiable — burying the system without inspection requires excavating at the owner's expense.

DIY installation in Arkansas — what's actually allowed

This is one of the most searched questions about Arkansas septic systems. The answer is limited. A homeowner can technically install a conventional system on their own property — but only after passing the ADH licensing examination and registering with the state, the same path a professional installer takes. For any alternative system (required throughout the Ozark karst region), homeowner installation is explicitly prohibited. A licensed Advanced Septic Installer must perform the work.

In practice, very few homeowners pursue self-installation because the licensing process requires passing a technical examination covering soils, hydraulics, and system design. The cost savings rarely justify the time and risk.

Arkansas questions

Arkansas septic questions answered

What are Arkansas septic system requirements?
Arkansas septic systems are regulated by the Arkansas Department of Health (ADH) under Act 402 of 1977. Minimum tank is 1,000 gallons. A licensed Designated Representative evaluates the soil and designs the system; a licensed Advanced Septic Installer performs the work. Two permits are required from the local county health unit: construction and operation. No work begins without the construction permit.
What is a Designated Representative in Arkansas?
A DR is an ADH-licensed professional — typically a Registered Professional Engineer, Sanitarian, Land Surveyor, or Licensed Master Plumber — who conducts soil evaluations and designs septic systems. The DR submits the permit application to the county sanitarian. Without a licensed DR preparing the design, no permit can be issued. Find one through the ADH licensee database searching "Designated Representative."
Why do Ozark karst areas need alternative septic systems?
The Ozark Plateau sits on fractured limestone bedrock. When conventional drainfield trenches are dug in karst terrain, effluent reaches the fractured rock without adequate soil treatment and flows directly into groundwater through rock cracks and fissures. This contamination risk is why ADH prohibits conventional systems in confirmed karst areas and requires aerobic treatment units, sand filters, or mound systems — all of which treat effluent more thoroughly before ground contact.
Can you install your own septic system in Arkansas?
Only under limited conditions. A homeowner must pass the ADH Advanced Septic Installer licensing examination and register with the state before self-installing a conventional system on their own property. For alternative systems — required throughout Ozark karst areas — homeowner installation is not permitted at all. In practice, very few homeowners pursue self-installation because the licensing process is equivalent to what professional installers must complete.
How much does a septic system cost in Arkansas?
Conventional systems in central Arkansas: $6,000 to $11,000. Ozark karst areas requiring aerobic or sand filter systems: $12,000 to $25,000. Ouachita mountain sites with shallow bedrock: $10,000 to $20,000. Delta clay areas: $8,000 to $15,000. DR evaluation adds $500 to $1,500. Karst and rocky terrain are the biggest cost drivers in Arkansas.
What permits are required for a septic system in Arkansas?
Two permits from the local county health unit: a construction permit before installation begins, and an operation permit after passing inspection. The Designated Representative submits the design as part of the construction permit application. The county sanitarian reviews and issues both permits under ADH oversight. No work may begin without the construction permit in hand.
Is a septic inspection required when selling a house in Arkansas?
No statewide requirement exists for inspection at property sale. ADH and EPA recommend pumping every three to five years. Buyers are responsible for their own due diligence — an inspection and pump-out before closing is strongly recommended, especially in Ozark karst areas where failing systems can affect groundwater and neighbouring properties.
