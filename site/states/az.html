The ground you are actually building on

Caliche is the defining problem — a cemented calcium carbonate layer that can be near-impenetrable and is common across central and southern Arizona. Elsewhere the state ranges from fast desert sands to tight clays, with shallow bedrock in the high country.

What gets installed in Arizona

Conventional trenches and chambers where soil allows. Where caliche or rock intervenes, designs move to alternative systems from Arizona's approved general permit list, which is more structured than most states'.

Getting a system permitted

Arizona uses a general permit system. Conventional systems fall under one general permit type, alternative technologies under others, and the applicable permit determines the design standard. A site investigation including soil profile and percolation testing is required.

  1. Soil evaluation. On the actual field location, before design. It decides what you are allowed to build.
  2. Design and site plan. Tank size, absorption area, and setbacks from wells, lines, water and foundations.
  3. Permit application. Submitted with the soil report. Allow weeks, more in the spring rush.
  4. Installation. By a licensed installer where Arizona requires one.
  5. Inspection before backfill. Trenches must be seen open.

Drainfield sizing in Arizona

A three-bedroom home at 150 gallons per bedroom produces 450 gallons per day of design flow. On sandy loam that needs roughly 750 square feet of absorption area. On clay loam it rises to about 2,250 square feet, and on clay a conventional field is usually not permitted at all. Run your own soil class through the drainfield calculator.

Maintenance and inspection

Arizona requires an inspection and report at property transfer for on-site systems, which is stricter than most states. Use the pump schedule calculator to work out the interval for your tank size and household.

Questions specific to Arizona

What is caliche and why does it matter for a septic system?
Caliche is a hardened layer of calcium carbonate found through much of Arizona's soil profile. It behaves like rock: water will not pass through it, and excavation can require a hammer attachment. If caliche sits at trench depth the site usually needs a design that works above it, and the excavation line on the quote rises sharply.
Does Arizona require a septic inspection when selling a house?
Yes. Arizona requires an inspection of the on-site wastewater facility and a report of transfer when a property with a septic system changes hands. Budget for it as part of a sale.

Verify before you build. The figures above are state minimums compiled for planning, drawn from Arizona Administrative Code Title 18, Chapter 9, Articles 1 through 3. County and health district rules routinely exceed them, code text changes, and only Arizona DEQ — On-site Wastewater Program or your local health department can confirm what applies to your parcel.
