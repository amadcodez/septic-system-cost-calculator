Colorado septic system requirements

Colorado calls septic systems OWTS — On-site Wastewater Treatment Systems. The state sets minimum standards through Regulation 43, updated in 2025-2026, but every permit comes from your county health department. Above 8,000 feet, engineered design is required — and mountain counties enforce some of the strictest OWTS rules in the country.

2025-2026 update

What changed in Colorado's updated Regulation 43

Colorado adopted a major Regulation 43 revision in March 2025. Counties are implementing updated local rules through 2026 — if you are planning a new system, confirm which version of the rules applies in your county.

Soil testing — now the foundation

Every design starts with soil evaluation

Updated Regulation 43 makes soil morphology evaluation the foundation of all OWTS design. Generic prescriptive sizing based on bedroom count alone is no longer sufficient in most Colorado counties. A certified professional must evaluate the actual soil profile — texture, structure, depth to restrictive layers, and seasonal high water table — before any design is prepared. This change affects both new installations and system replacements.

Operating permits — new requirement

Higher-level treatment units need operating permits

Effective June 2026, systems that include higher-level treatment units — nitrogen-reducing systems (NDDS) and similar technologies — require an operating permit in addition to the construction permit. Systems with only pumps or lift stations are exempt. This primarily affects mountain counties where nitrogen management near sensitive water bodies is required, but also applies to certain Front Range counties near reservoirs.

County implementation

Each county adopts its own version

CDPHE's updated Regulation 43 is the minimum — counties adopt local rules that are as strict or stricter. Gilpin County implemented updated rules in March 2026. Elbert County updated June 2026. Summit, Eagle, and Pitkin counties have had stricter-than-state rules for years and are updating further. Check with your specific county health department for the version of Regulation 43 that applies to your permit.

Setbacks — updated distances

100 feet from wells, more near water

Colorado requires a minimum 100-foot setback from private wells — stricter than many states. Setbacks from streams, lakes, and reservoirs are increased under the updated regulation, particularly in mountain counties near ski resort communities and sensitive watersheds. Lot size and the ability to meet setbacks is often the binding constraint on small mountain properties, independent of soil conditions.

Elevation matters

Colorado OWTS by elevation zone — three different design worlds

No other state has elevation as a formal design trigger in its regulations. Colorado's terrain creates three distinct OWTS environments.

Below 6,000 feet

Front Range and eastern plains

The Denver metro area, Colorado Springs, Pueblo, and the eastern plains sit below 6,000 feet. Soil conditions vary — clay-heavy soils dominate much of the Front Range, while the plains have deeper silty loam. Conventional gravity OWTS are common where soil percolation allows. Jefferson, Douglas, El Paso, and Larimer counties are the most active permitting counties in this zone. Frost depth is a consideration but not the dominant design constraint.

6,000 to 8,000 feet

Foothills and transition zone

The foothills west of Denver — Gilpin, Clear Creek, Teller, and parts of Jefferson and Park counties — sit in the transition zone. Soils are shallower and rockier than the Front Range. Frost depth increases significantly. Conventional systems are still possible in favorable locations, but engineered designs are more common. Many properties in this zone have granite bedrock close to the surface, limiting trench depth and pushing costs upward.

Above 8,000 feet

Mountain counties — engineered design required

Summit, Eagle, Pitkin, Grand, and high-elevation portions of other counties require engineered designs for all OWTS. Shallow soils over granite, frost depths of 4 to 6 feet, short construction seasons, and proximity to sensitive mountain watersheds combine to make standard prescriptive approaches inadequate. Summit County (Breckenridge, Keystone) and Pitkin County (Aspen) have some of the strictest OWTS requirements in Colorado — and the highest installation costs.

Getting a Colorado OWTS permitted

Colorado permitting runs entirely through county health departments — CDPHE sets the rules, counties issue permits. The process is similar across counties but local requirements and timelines vary.

Step 1 — Soil evaluation by a certified professional

Under updated Regulation 43, soil evaluation is the first and most critical step. A certified professional evaluates the site: soil texture, structure, depth to bedrock or seasonal high water table, and percolation characteristics. In mountain counties above 8,000 feet, this step also confirms whether any conventional system is feasible. Cost: $300 to $900 on the Front Range, $800 to $2,500 in mountain counties where engineered evaluations are required.

Step 2 — System design

Based on the soil evaluation, a design is prepared. Below 8,000 feet with favorable soil, a standard prescriptive design following Regulation 43 tables is often acceptable. Above 8,000 feet, or on sites with challenging soil conditions, a stamped engineering design is required. Mountain county designs require a Registered Professional Engineer — not just a certified installer or evaluator.

Step 3 — Permit application to county health department

Submit the soil evaluation, system design, and site plan to your county health department with the permit fee. Permit fees range from $300 to $1,000 depending on county. Review times vary — Front Range counties typically take two to four weeks; mountain counties during peak season can run longer. Gilpin and Elbert counties updated their local rules in 2026 — confirm the current application requirements before submitting.

Step 4 — Installation by a licensed contractor

Colorado requires a licensed OWTS installer. Search the CDPHE database for state-certified installers in your area. The installer must follow the approved design — field changes require county approval before proceeding.

Step 5 — Inspection before backfill

The county health department must inspect the open system before backfill. Contact your county to schedule — in mountain counties, inspector availability during the short construction season can create scheduling delays. Backfilling without inspection requires excavating at the owner's expense.

OWTS licensing in Colorado

Colorado requires separate licenses for different OWTS roles. Search the CDPHE online database for state-certified septic system installers and pumpers. Mountain counties requiring engineered designs effectively mandate a Registered Professional Engineer for design work — verify what your specific county requires before hiring.

Colorado Springs septic systems

El Paso County — which includes Colorado Springs — is the most populous Colorado county relying significantly on OWTS. Clay soils dominate much of the area, which affects drainfield sizing. El Paso County Public Health requires a Use Permit inspection for property transfers involving OWTS — buyers and sellers should plan for this requirement before closing. Colorado Springs contractors are generally experienced with the local clay soil conditions and El Paso County permit process.

Colorado questions

Colorado OWTS questions answered

What is Regulation 43 in Colorado?
Regulation 43 is Colorado's statewide OWTS standard, administered by CDPHE. It sets minimum requirements for siting, design, construction, and operation of systems treating 2,000 gallons per day or less. A major update was adopted March 2025 with counties implementing through 2026. County health departments use Regulation 43 as a floor — they can and frequently do adopt stricter local requirements.
What does OWTS mean in Colorado?
OWTS stands for On-site Wastewater Treatment System — Colorado's official term for a septic system. The drainfield is called a Soil Treatment Area (STA). These terms appear on all Colorado permit applications, design documents, and county health department forms. If you are looking for a "septic permit" in Colorado, ask for an OWTS permit at your county health department.
Do Colorado systems above 8,000 feet need engineered designs?
Yes. Colorado requires a stamped engineering design for all OWTS above 8,000 feet elevation. Shallow soils, deep frost, bedrock limitations, and cold temperatures that slow biological treatment make standard prescriptive designs inadequate. Summit, Eagle, Pitkin, Grand, and high-elevation portions of other counties enforce this requirement and typically add further local requirements on top.
How much does a septic system cost in Colorado?
Front Range and eastern plains: $8,000 to $16,000. Colorado Springs area: $10,000 to $18,000. Foothills: $14,000 to $25,000. Mountain counties above 8,000 feet: $20,000 to $40,000. Pitkin County (Aspen): $25,000 to $50,000 or more. Elevation and county requirements are the primary cost drivers in Colorado.
Who issues septic permits in Colorado?
County health departments issue OWTS permits — not CDPHE directly. CDPHE sets minimum standards through Regulation 43, but all permitting, inspection, and enforcement is county-level. Each of Colorado's 64 counties may have requirements stricter than the state standard. Contact your specific county health department for permit applications, fees, and current local requirements.
Is a septic inspection required when selling a house in Colorado?
No statewide requirement exists, but most counties require a Use Permit or inspection before property transfer. El Paso County (Colorado Springs), Jefferson County, and many mountain counties have local transfer inspection requirements. Check with your specific county health department — the requirements vary significantly across Colorado's 64 counties.
What changed in Colorado's 2026 Regulation 43 update?
Key changes include: soil testing made mandatory foundation of all design; new operating permit requirements for systems with treatment units (effective June 2026 in some counties); updated setback standards; and stricter requirements for nitrogen-reducing systems near sensitive water bodies. Each county implemented its own updated local rules — Gilpin County in March 2026, Elbert County in June 2026, with others following throughout 2026.
