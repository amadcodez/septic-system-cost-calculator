The ground you are actually building on

Coastal plain sands on the Eastern Shore and southern Maryland, piedmont clay through the centre, and shallow rock in the west. Chesapeake Bay nitrogen loading dominates the regulatory conversation.

What gets installed in Maryland

Conventional trenches and sand mounds. Best Available Technology for nitrogen removal is required in the Critical Area and in other designated zones — a defining feature of Maryland's program.

Getting a system permitted

Maryland requires Best Available Technology nitrogen-reducing systems for new construction in the Chesapeake Bay Critical Area and in certain other circumstances. The Bay Restoration Fund has historically offered grants toward BAT upgrades.

  1. Soil evaluation. On the actual field location, before design. It decides what you are allowed to build.
  2. Design and site plan. Tank size, absorption area, and setbacks from wells, lines, water and foundations.
  3. Permit application. Submitted with the soil report. Allow weeks, more in the spring rush.
  4. Installation. By a licensed installer where Maryland requires one.
  5. Inspection before backfill. Trenches must be seen open.

Drainfield sizing in Maryland

A three-bedroom home at 150 gallons per bedroom produces 450 gallons per day of design flow. On sandy loam that needs roughly 750 square feet of absorption area. On clay loam it rises to about 2,250 square feet, and on clay a conventional field is usually not permitted at all. Run your own soil class through the drainfield calculator.

Questions specific to Maryland

What is BAT and when does Maryland require it?
Best Available Technology for nitrogen removal — an advanced treatment unit that cuts the nitrogen reaching groundwater and ultimately the Chesapeake Bay. Maryland requires it for new systems in the Critical Area near tidal waters and in certain other designated situations. It raises both installation cost and ongoing maintenance obligations.
Is there financial help for a BAT upgrade?
Maryland's Bay Restoration Fund has historically provided grant assistance toward nitrogen-reducing upgrades. Availability and terms change, so check with your county health department and MDE before assuming a figure.

Verify before you build. The figures above are state minimums compiled for planning, drawn from COMAR 26.04.02, Onsite Sewage Disposal Systems. County and health district rules routinely exceed them, code text changes, and only Maryland DOE — Onsite Systems Division or your local health department can confirm what applies to your parcel.
