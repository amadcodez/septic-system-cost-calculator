Maine's unique system

The Licensed Site Evaluator — Maine's biggest difference

Most states have a health department evaluator assess your soil, then a separate designer creates the system plan. Maine combines both into one licensed professional.

What an LSE does

Evaluation + design in one step

The LSE digs test pits, reads the soil profile, determines the seasonal high water table, selects the appropriate system type, sizes the components, and prepares the HHE-200 application — all in a single engagement. You hire the LSE before the installer, not after.

The HHE-200 form

Maine's permit application

The HHE-200 is the document your LSE prepares that covers the soil evaluation, system design, site plan, and all required setbacks. The Local Plumbing Inspector reviews it and issues the permit. No HHE-200 means no permit — and no permit means no backfill approval.

How to find one

Maine DHHS LSE registry

Maine DHHS maintains a registry of licensed LSEs. Search by county — local LSEs know the local inspectors, the common soil patterns, and what the LPI expects to see on the HHE-200. Using an out-of-area LSE unfamiliar with local conditions is a common mistake on vacation property purchases.

Cost of an LSE evaluation

$400 to $1,000 typically

Complex coastal or ledge sites run higher. The LSE fee covers evaluation and the HHE-200 preparation — some LSEs bundle design review with the installer, others work independently. Clarify scope before engaging. See perc test costs by state for comparison.

Maine geology

What the ground looks like in Maine — and why it matters

Maine's geology is dominated by one fact: glacial till over granite bedrock. Ledge depth is the single biggest variable in Maine septic design and cost.

Inland Maine

Glacial till — variable ledge depth

Most of inland Maine sits on glacial till deposited during the last ice age — a mix of silt, clay, sand, and rocks of varying sizes. Beneath it, granite bedrock appears at unpredictable depths. A site might have 6 feet of workable till; a neighbor 50 feet away might hit ledge at 18 inches. The LSE's test pits determine which situation you have before any money is spent on installation.

Coastal Maine

Shallow ledge + small lots + high water table

Coastal areas combine the worst of every constraint: ledge near the surface limits trench depth; small lot sizes restrict where the field can go; shoreline setbacks and wetland buffers remove more area; and seasonal high water tables near the ocean push designs upward into fill systems. This is why coastal Maine systems routinely cost two to three times the inland equivalent.

Islands

All the coastal constraints plus marine logistics

Island sites on Deer Isle, Mount Desert, Vinalhaven, and others face every coastal constraint plus the cost of moving heavy equipment by ferry or barge. Material costs rise, scheduling windows narrow, and contractor availability is limited. A $15,000 system on the mainland can run $30,000 to $50,000 on a remote island.

Sandy coastal areas

Good drainage — but water table constraints

Some coastal areas — particularly parts of York County and the southern coast — have sandy outwash soils that drain well. The perc rate is favorable, but the seasonal high water table near the ocean often requires elevated systems to maintain required separation. Good soil does not automatically mean an easy design in Maine.

Getting a septic system permitted in Maine

Maine's permit process is more streamlined than most states because the LSE handles both evaluation and design. But it requires engaging the right professional in the right order.

Step 1 — Hire a Licensed Site Evaluator

This is your first call — before an installer, before a contractor, before anything. The LSE evaluates the site and designs the system. Find a licensed LSE through Maine DHHS. Local LSEs who regularly work with your Local Plumbing Inspector are worth seeking out — familiarity with local standards speeds review.

Step 2 — Site evaluation and HHE-200 preparation

The LSE digs test pits, determines ledge depth and seasonal high water table, classifies the soil, selects the system type, and completes the HHE-200 application. On ledge-prone sites this step can take multiple visits. Cost: $400 to $1,000 for most sites; more on complex coastal or island parcels.

Step 3 — Local Plumbing Inspector review and permit

The HHE-200 goes to your Local Plumbing Inspector (LPI) — a municipal official, not a state employee. The LPI reviews for compliance with Chapter 241 and issues the permit. Review time varies by municipality; coastal towns in summer can take longer due to volume.

Step 4 — Installation by a licensed plumber

Maine requires a licensed plumber for septic installation — not just a general contractor or excavator. The installer must hold the appropriate Maine plumbing license. Verify license status before signing any contract.

Step 5 — Inspection before backfill

The LPI must inspect the open system before backfill. This is non-negotiable. Backfilling without inspection means excavating again at your expense. Schedule the inspection before the installer plans to cover the work.

Maintenance in Maine

Maine does not set a mandatory statewide pumping interval. The standard recommendation is every three to five years for a family of four. Maine's cold climate slows bacterial digestion, which means sludge can accumulate faster than in warmer states — erring toward three years rather than five is common advice for year-round homes. Seasonal or vacation properties that sit unused for months need pumping assessed differently — consult your installer.

Maine questions

Maine septic questions answered

What is the minimum septic tank size in Maine?
Maine requires a minimum 1,000 gallon tank for a one to three bedroom dwelling under Chapter 241. Design flow is 150 gallons per bedroom per day, held two days. In practice, Licensed Site Evaluators frequently specify larger tanks based on site conditions, seasonal use patterns, and Maine's cold climate, which slows bacterial digestion.
What is a Licensed Site Evaluator in Maine?
An LSE is a state-licensed professional who both evaluates your soil and designs your septic system — combined into a single step. In Maine you hire the LSE before the installer. The LSE prepares the HHE-200 application, which is your permit application. This differs from most states where soil evaluation and system design are handled by separate parties or the health department.
How much does a septic system cost in Maine?
Inland rural sites: $10,000 to $16,000. Central and southern Maine: $12,000 to $24,000. Coastal mainland: $18,000 to $32,000. Islands: $25,000 to $50,000 or more. Ledge rock excavation, imported fill over shallow ledge, and marine logistics on island sites are the main cost drivers above the inland baseline.
What is the HHE-200 form?
The HHE-200 is Maine's subsurface wastewater disposal system application, prepared by your Licensed Site Evaluator. It documents the soil evaluation results, system design, site plan, and required setbacks. The Local Plumbing Inspector reviews the HHE-200 and issues the permit. No permit can be issued without a completed HHE-200 from a licensed LSE.
Why are coastal Maine septic systems so expensive?
Coastal sites combine multiple cost factors: ledge rock near the surface requires hammer excavation or blasting; small lot sizes limit field placement options; shoreline and wetland setbacks remove more potential field area; and seasonal high water tables require elevated fill systems. Island sites add marine logistics — equipment and materials travel by ferry or barge. Each factor adds cost independently; coastal sites often face all of them together.
Does Maine require a septic inspection when selling a house?
There is no statewide mandatory inspection at property transfer in Maine. However, many mortgage lenders require a septic inspection before financing. Some coastal municipalities have adopted local requirements. It is strongly recommended to have any system inspected before purchase — discovering a failed system after closing is expensive with limited recourse.
How often should a Maine septic tank be pumped?
Every three to five years for a year-round household of four on a 1,000 gallon tank. Maine's cold climate slows biological breakdown in the tank, so erring toward three years rather than five is common advice for year-round homes. Seasonal properties need a different assessment based on months of use. Use the pump schedule calculator for your specific household size and tank capacity.
