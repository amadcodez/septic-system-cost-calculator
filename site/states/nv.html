The ground you are actually building on

Desert soils that range from very fast-draining alluvial sand and gravel to caliche-cemented layers. Deep groundwater in most areas eases separation requirements. Rock is common in the mountain areas.

What gets installed in Nevada

Conventional trenches and seepage pits, the latter still permitted in circumstances many states have abandoned. Advanced treatment where nitrogen loading to a shallow aquifer is a concern.

Getting a system permitted

The two urban health authorities — Southern Nevada Health District covering Clark County and Washoe County Health District — have their own requirements, and they cover most of the state's population. Rural counties fall under NDEP.

  1. Soil evaluation. On the actual field location, before design. It decides what you are allowed to build.
  2. Design and site plan. Tank size, absorption area, and setbacks from wells, lines, water and foundations.
  3. Permit application. Submitted with the soil report. Allow weeks, more in the spring rush.
  4. Installation. By a licensed installer where Nevada requires one.
  5. Inspection before backfill. Trenches must be seen open.

Drainfield sizing in Nevada

A three-bedroom home at 150 gallons per bedroom produces 450 gallons per day of design flow. On sandy loam that needs roughly 750 square feet of absorption area. On clay loam it rises to about 2,250 square feet, and on clay a conventional field is usually not permitted at all. Run your own soil class through the drainfield calculator.

Questions specific to Nevada

Are seepage pits allowed in Nevada?
They remain permitted in some Nevada jurisdictions where deep, permeable soil and a very deep water table make them viable — a design many states have prohibited entirely. Whether one is allowed on your site depends on the local health authority and the depth to groundwater.
Why does Pahrump have septic restrictions?
Nye County's Pahrump valley has a very large concentration of septic systems over a groundwater basin that supplies drinking water, and nitrate accumulation has been a documented concern. Requirements there reflect that, and conversion pressure exists in parts of the valley.

Verify before you build. The figures above are state minimums compiled for planning, drawn from Nevada Administrative Code Chapter 444, Individual Sewage Disposal Systems. County and health district rules routinely exceed them, code text changes, and only Nevada DEP — Bureau of Water Pollution Control or your local health department can confirm what applies to your parcel.
