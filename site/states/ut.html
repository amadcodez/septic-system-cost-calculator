Utah septic system requirements

Utah sets a 1,000 gallon minimum tank and 150 gallons per bedroom per day design flow — but the real variables are your county health department, your soil, and whether your site is in a canyon, a valley, or near a sensitive water body.

Held two days minimum for settling before discharge to the drainfield.

Soil conditions

What the ground actually looks like in Utah

Utah's geology varies more than almost any other state — your county and site location determine your system type and cost more than the state rule does.

Wasatch Front valleys

Salt Lake, Utah, Davis counties

Coarse alluvial soils deposited by ancient Lake Bonneville drain quickly — often too quickly. Fast percolation rates mean effluent can reach groundwater before being treated. Many Wasatch Front sites require pressure distribution or enhanced treatment to protect the valley aquifer. Water table depth varies significantly across the valley floor.

Cache Valley

Cache County — Logan area

Lake-bottom clays left by prehistoric Lake Bonneville are tight and slow-draining. Percolation rates through Cache Valley clay are among the slowest in Utah, pushing drainfield sizes well above the state minimum. Pressure distribution is commonly required. The seasonal high water table in low-lying areas further constrains design.

Canyon and mountain areas

Summit, Wasatch, Morgan counties

Shallow bedrock — granite, quartzite, and limestone — limits trench depth and drives up excavation costs dramatically. Hammer excavation or blasting is sometimes required. Summit County (Park City) enforces strict environmental standards on top of the physical difficulty. Short construction seasons at elevation add scheduling constraints and contractor premiums.

Washington County

St. George — Hurricane — Ivins

Desert soils with caliche layers. Caliche is a cemented calcium carbonate layer that behaves like rock — impenetrable to water and requiring mechanical breaking. Where caliche sits at trench depth, conventional installation is slow and expensive. Desert soils themselves can drain fast or be layered with clay; a perc test on the specific parcel is essential before budgeting.

Heber Valley

Wasatch County

A high mountain valley with a shallow water table that rises seasonally. Wasatch County Health Department enforces groundwater separation requirements strictly, and many Heber Valley sites need elevated or mound systems to achieve adequate separation. Growth pressure has intensified permitting scrutiny in recent years.

Uinta Basin

Duchesne, Uintah counties

Semi-arid soils with variable percolation. Groundwater is generally deeper here than along the Wasatch Front, which eases separation requirements. However, caliche and clay layers appear in places. Rural lot sizes are typically larger, which helps with setback compliance.

Getting a septic system permitted in Utah

Utah permitting runs through your local health department, not the state directly. The process follows R317-4 but each county adds its own scheduling, form requirements, and inspection standards.

Step 1 — Soil evaluation and perc test

Before any design work, a soil evaluation establishes what your site can support. Utah accepts percolation tests but most counties now prefer or require a soil morphology evaluation — a licensed evaluator reads the soil profile and determines the seasonal high water table. This step decides your system type and field size. Cost: $250 to $700 in most Utah counties; Summit County engineered evaluations run higher.

Step 2 — System design

A design meeting R317-4 requirements is prepared based on the evaluation. For complex sites — canyon areas, high water table locations, sensitive watersheds — Summit and Wasatch counties typically require a stamped engineering design rather than a standard prescriptive design.

Step 3 — Permit application

Submitted to your county health department with the soil evaluation report, site plan, and design. Utah County and Summit County applications require additional documentation for sensitive area sites. Budget two to four weeks for review; Summit County reviews can run longer.

Step 4 — Installation by a licensed contractor

Utah requires a licensed onsite wastewater installer. Homeowner installation is permitted on owner-occupied property in some circumstances but requires prior health department approval. In Summit and Wasatch counties, inspector notification requirements before breaking ground are enforced.

Step 5 — Inspection before backfill

The local health department must inspect the open trenches and tank before any backfill. This is non-negotiable statewide. Backfilling before inspection results in an order to excavate — at the owner's expense.

Maintenance and pumping in Utah

Utah does not set a mandatory statewide pumping interval for conventional residential systems. The standard recommendation is every three to five years for a family of four on a 1,000 gallon tank. Summit County and Wasatch County advanced systems carry operation and maintenance agreements with reporting to the health department. Utah questions

Utah septic questions answered

What is the minimum septic tank size in Utah?
Utah requires a minimum 1,000 gallon tank for a one to three bedroom dwelling under R317-4. Design flow is 150 gallons per bedroom per day, held two days. Local health departments — particularly in Summit and Wasatch counties — may require larger tanks based on site-specific conditions or advanced treatment requirements.
How much does a septic system cost in Utah?
Cost varies dramatically by location. Wasatch Front valleys: $8,000 to $14,000 for a conventional system. Utah Valley: $9,000 to $16,000. Park City and Summit County: $18,000 to $35,000 or more. Heber Valley mound systems: $14,000 to $22,000. St. George area: $9,000 to $18,000. Rock excavation, advanced treatment requirements, and short mountain construction seasons are the biggest cost drivers above the state baseline.
Who issues septic permits in Utah?
Your local health department, not the state directly. Salt Lake County Health Department, Utah County Environmental Health, Summit County Health Department, Southwest Utah Public Health (Washington County), and Bear River Health (Cache County) each administer R317-4 for their area and can set requirements above the state minimum.
Why does a septic system cost so much in Park City?
Three factors: shallow granite and quartzite bedrock requires hammer excavation or blasting; Summit County enforces strict environmental requirements including advanced treatment in many areas; and the short high-elevation construction season means fewer contractor days and higher scheduling premiums. A system costing $10,000 in the valley routinely runs $20,000 to $30,000 or more in Summit County.
Can I install a septic system myself in Utah?
Utah permits homeowner installation on owner-occupied property, but prior approval from the local health department is required, the design must meet R317-4, and the installation must pass inspection before backfill. Complex sites and most canyon or sensitive area locations effectively require a licensed installer regardless of the general homeowner allowance.
Are there special septic rules near Utah Lake?
Yes. Utah County Environmental Health enforces enhanced setbacks and, in some areas, nitrogen management requirements for systems near Utah Lake. The lake is a sensitive water body and its watershed receives additional regulatory attention. Contact Utah County Environmental Health directly for the current rules affecting your specific parcel near the lake.
What soil type is common on the Wasatch Front?
Coarse alluvial soils deposited by glacial-era Lake Bonneville. These drain quickly — application rates of 0.80 gallons per day per square foot are common — which means smaller drainfields than tighter soils, but also faster movement of effluent toward groundwater. Some Wasatch Front sites require pressure distribution or enhanced treatment specifically because of how fast the soil drains.
