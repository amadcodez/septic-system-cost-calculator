The ground you are actually building on

Glacial till over bedrock with ledge at shallow depth across much of the state, steep slopes, and seasonal high groundwater. Clay soils in the Champlain valley.

What gets installed in Vermont

Conventional trenches where soil depth allows. Mounds and at-grade systems are common. Vermont permits innovative and alternative technologies through a specific approval pathway.

Getting a system permitted

Vermont requires a licensed designer and issues a combined Wastewater System and Potable Water Supply Permit — the water supply and wastewater approvals are handled together, which differs from most states. Design flow is calculated at a lower rate per bedroom than the national norm.

  1. Soil evaluation. On the actual field location, before design. It decides what you are allowed to build.
  2. Design and site plan. Tank size, absorption area, and setbacks from wells, lines, water and foundations.
  3. Permit application. Submitted with the soil report. Allow weeks, more in the spring rush.
  4. Installation. By a licensed installer where Vermont requires one.
  5. Inspection before backfill. Trenches must be seen open.

Drainfield sizing in Vermont

A three-bedroom home at 140 gallons per bedroom produces 420 gallons per day of design flow. On sandy loam that needs roughly 700 square feet of absorption area. On clay loam it rises to about 2,100 square feet, and on clay a conventional field is usually not permitted at all. Run your own soil class through the drainfield calculator.

Maintenance and inspection

Questions specific to Vermont

What is a combined water and wastewater permit in Vermont?
Vermont issues a single Wastewater System and Potable Water Supply Permit covering both the septic system and the drinking water source. Because well setbacks and septic placement interact, handling them in one application makes sense, but it means a licensed designer must address both together.
Why is Vermont's design flow lower per bedroom?
Vermont's rules calculate design flow at a rate below the 150 gallons per bedroom used in most states, reflecting measured household water use. It results in modestly smaller required capacity for the same bedroom count than a neighbouring state would demand.

Verify before you build. The figures above are state minimums compiled for planning, drawn from Vermont Wastewater System and Potable Water Supply Rules. County and health district rules routinely exceed them, code text changes, and only Vermont DEC — Drinking Water and Groundwater Protection or your local health department can confirm what applies to your parcel.
