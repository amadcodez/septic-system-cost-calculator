The ground you are actually building on

Glacial till with a hardpan layer across the Puget Sound lowlands, outwash sand in places, volcanic soils in the Cascades, and loess in the Palouse. Winter rainfall keeps water tables high on the west side.

What gets installed in Washington

Conventional gravity systems where soil allows, but pressure distribution, sand filters and mounds are extremely common on till and hardpan sites. Treatment standards are tiered by soil type.

Getting a system permitted

Washington requires an operation and maintenance program in the Puget Sound counties, including periodic inspections at intervals set by system type and reporting to the local health jurisdiction. Property owners are responsible for keeping the system inspected.

  1. Soil evaluation. On the actual field location, before design. It decides what you are allowed to build.
  2. Design and site plan. Tank size, absorption area, and setbacks from wells, lines, water and foundations.
  3. Permit application. Submitted with the soil report. Allow weeks, more in the spring rush.
  4. Installation. By a licensed installer where Washington requires one.
  5. Inspection before backfill. Trenches must be seen open.

Drainfield sizing in Washington

A three-bedroom home at 120 gallons per bedroom produces 360 gallons per day of design flow. On sandy loam that needs roughly 600 square feet of absorption area. On clay loam it rises to about 1,800 square feet, and on clay a conventional field is usually not permitted at all. Run your own soil class through the drainfield calculator.

Maintenance and inspection

Puget Sound counties require periodic inspection — commonly annually for advanced systems and every three years for gravity systems — with reports filed to the health jurisdiction. Use the pump schedule calculator to work out the interval for your tank size and household.

Questions specific to Washington

What are Washington's O&M inspection requirements?
In the twelve Puget Sound counties, homeowners must have their system inspected on a set schedule and file the report with the local health jurisdiction — commonly every three years for a conventional gravity system and annually for systems with pumps or treatment components. It is an ongoing legal obligation, not a recommendation, and non-compliance can be enforced.
What is hardpan and how does it affect design?
A dense, cemented glacial till layer found across the Puget Sound lowlands that water will not readily pass. It creates a perched water table above it during the wet season. Because Washington requires separation from that limiting layer, sites with shallow hardpan need shallow pressure-distributed systems, sand filters or mounds rather than deep gravity trenches.

Verify before you build. The figures above are state minimums compiled for planning, drawn from WAC 246-272A, On-Site Sewage Systems. County and health district rules routinely exceed them, code text changes, and only Washington State DOH — Wastewater Management Section or your local health department can confirm what applies to your parcel.
