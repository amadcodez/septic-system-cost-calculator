The ground you are actually building on

Semi-arid soils with wide variation — coarse alluvium in the river valleys, clay in the basins, and shallow bedrock across much of the state. Groundwater is generally deep, which eases separation.

What gets installed in Wyoming

Conventional trenches and seepage pits where soil depth allows. Large lot sizes make lagoons viable on some rural properties. Advanced treatment is required where groundwater protection warrants it.

Getting a system permitted

Wyoming DEQ issues small wastewater permits, and several counties administer delegated programs. Lot sizes are large enough that setbacks are rarely the binding constraint — soil depth and rock usually are.

  1. Soil evaluation. On the actual field location, before design. It decides what you are allowed to build.
  2. Design and site plan. Tank size, absorption area, and setbacks from wells, lines, water and foundations.
  3. Permit application. Submitted with the soil report. Allow weeks, more in the spring rush.
  4. Installation. By a licensed installer where Wyoming requires one.
  5. Inspection before backfill. Trenches must be seen open.

Drainfield sizing in Wyoming

A three-bedroom home at 150 gallons per bedroom produces 450 gallons per day of design flow. On sandy loam that needs roughly 750 square feet of absorption area. On clay loam it rises to about 2,250 square feet, and on clay a conventional field is usually not permitted at all. Run your own soil class through the drainfield calculator.

Questions specific to Wyoming

Why is Teton County so much more expensive?
Jackson Hole combines a high water table near the Snake River, strict environmental protection requirements, a short construction season, extremely high labour costs and difficult access. A septic system there can cost several times the same design in eastern Wyoming.
Are seepage pits permitted in Wyoming?
In areas with deep, permeable soil and a very deep water table, they remain a possibility, though trenches are the standard approach. The local permitting authority — DEQ or a delegated county — determines whether the site conditions support one.

Verify before you build. The figures above are state minimums compiled for planning, drawn from Wyoming Water Quality Rules Chapter 25, Small Wastewater Systems. County and health district rules routinely exceed them, code text changes, and only Wyoming DEQ — Water Quality Division, Small Wastewater or your local health department can confirm what applies to your parcel.
